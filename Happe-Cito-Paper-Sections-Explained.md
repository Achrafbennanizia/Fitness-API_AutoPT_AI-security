# Section-by-section explanation of Happe & Cito (ESEC/FSE 2023)

Paper title: *Getting pwn’d by AI: Penetration Testing with Large Language Models* (Andreas Happe and Jürgen Cito, TU Wien). Venue: ESEC/FSE ’23, pp. 2082–2086. DOI 10.1145/3611643.3613083. Also arXiv:2308.00121.

This is a five-page **ideas-plus-prototype** paper, not a 13-framework bake-off. It is a reading companion, not an attack playbook. They ask whether GPT-3.5-class models can be **sparring partners** for human testers: high-level planning, and a low-level closed loop against an **authorized vulnerable VM**.

**What this paper supports in the semester project:** early peer-reviewed evidence that LLMs can drive security-testing *steps*. Useful to justify “AI-assisted testing” even though your T3 is a **new** small agent: Happe is the ancestor, Deng is the HITL architecture, Peng is the warning that swarms are not free.

Word target: **300–500 words per subsection.**

## Abstract

The abstract is a problem statement plus two experiments plus an ethics flag. Penetration testing still eats specialist hours. LLMs (they name GPT-3.5) might become **sparring partners**: colleagues who suggest another idea when a tester is stuck. They split that hope into two use cases. **High-level:** help plan an assignment (what tactics even exist for this kind of job). **Low-level:** hunt inside one already-accessed lab VM. For the second, they wired GPT-3.5 to a vulnerable Linux guest over SSH: the model proposes a command, the command runs, output returns, repeat. They report that this loop could reach a root shell on that *deliberately broken* guest, then they spend the rest of the paper on instability, hallucinations, safety filters, and whether defenders should care.

The abstract is not a claim that pentesting is solved. It is a claim that the *shape* of future tools will include LLM loops, for good and for dual-use. ESEC/FSE accepted it as a short paper: evidence is preliminary, ethics is mandatory. For a master’s student, the sentence to steal is “augment existing human testers,” not “replace them with overnight swarms.” Your HITL agent is that sparring partner. Your overnight loop is the thing they already worry about in §4.3 and §6.

They also mention, then refuse, two other LLM uses: generating phishing/vishing copy, and auto-writing pentest reports. The first is declined on ethical grounds inside the paper. The second they note anecdotally that practitioners already try. Your proposal similarly refuses social-engineering content and keeps the report as a human-authored 20–30 pages with tables.

## 1 Introduction

The introduction is a labour-market paragraph plus a research question plus a scope fence. ISC2’s 2022 workforce study is cited: headcount grew ~11% year on year while the *gap* grew ~26%. Testers in an interview study (their own related FSE work) asked for **human sparring partners** and said intuition often comes from CTFs. The authors’ wager: some of that intuition can be queried from a model, which would raise the floor for novices (they cite evidence that generative AI helps lower-skilled workers more) and would keep a **human in the loop**, which they treat as an ethical mitigator.

The research question is blunt: **to what extent can we automate security testing with LLMs?** They do not answer with a 182-sub-task bench (that is Deng, a year later). They answer with structured anecdote: ATT&CK as the coverage ideal for a sparring partner; AgentGPT/AutoGPT for high-level plans; a Python SSH loop for low-level privilege escalation on `lin.security`.

Scope is as important as the RQ. They **will not** study LLM phishing. They mention report generation only as industry gossip. The paper you are reading is about **planning and lab execution assistance**, not about deceiving humans. Your semester ethics page can cite this fence: even a 2023 short paper already refused the ugliest dual-use slice.

ATT&CK is introduced as a *grading rubric for a colleague*, not as a runtime. A good sparring partner should speak tactics, then techniques, then procedures. High-level experiments test the first two. Low-level experiments test the last. Your label table (OWASP API + one ATT&CK technique per confirmed finding) is this idea after the campaign, not a Caldera emulation plan.

### MITRE ATT&CK (in §2)

ATT&CK is a curated knowledge base of how real threat actors behave. **Tactics** are goals (recon, privilege escalation, collection). **Techniques** are ways to serve a tactic (they give elevation-control and Kerberos-related *names* as examples of the catalogue, not as homework). **Procedures** are the messy specifics of a particular actor. The authors’ hypothesis: a sparring partner must cover that whole stack. High-level chat should pick plausible tactics/techniques for a scenario. Low-level chat, given a tactic, should propose feasible techniques/procedures on *this* host.

Your project does **not** run ATT&CK as a control plane. Peng already told you those models are labels. Happe tells you why a supervisor might still want the labels: so the agent is not only “vibes about security,” but can be scored against a public catalogue. On fitness APIs the matching techniques will often be things like T1190, T1078, T1530, T1552 — or “no close match.” That empty cell is allowed.

### 3.1 High-level: task-planning systems

They asked AgentGPT to plan a domain-admin path in Active Directory. The plan listed techniques that, the first author says from ten years of practice, are the ones consultants actually try (spraying, Kerberos-related attacks, AD CS, delegation, GPOs — named as *classes* of real work, not as a procedure dump). Then, with a **company’s approval**, they asked AutoGPT to draft an *external* pentest plan. The plan was boring in a good way: vuln scan, OSINT/user enum, phishing. When pushed, AutoGPT crawled the public website for possible email targets and **refused** to scan or phish live, thanks to ethical filters.

Read this as: LLMs already know the **genre** of a pentest plan. That is useful for a junior writing a scoping document. It is not evidence they can execute the plan. The approved-company anecdote is also your ethics template: even a planning demo wanted a letter. Your five Docker apps on `127.0.0.1` are the stricter version of that letter.

For the semester, 3.1 supports the **definition session** in which the agent names one OWASP API class for the fitness family. That is a planning act. You then refuse to take the name as success until HTTP evidence appears — Happe’s own next subsection shows why.

### 3.2 Low-level: attack-execution system

This is the SSH closed loop against the public `lin.security` training VM, implemented as `hackingBuddyGPT`. Infinite loop: model is told it is a low-privilege user who wants root; it emits a shell command; the command runs over SSH; stdout returns; optionally the model is asked to name weakness *classes* and “verification commands.” They report the loop **routinely** reached root on that lab image. Common *families* of path (sudoers misconfig, world-readable credential files, SUID curiosity that they did **not** successfully chain) are discussed as observations about the *model’s habits*, not as a tutorial. Reverse-shell phrasing in the prompt also produced shells. That last sentence is why overnight unattended loops are ethically heavier than HITL.

You must **not** reproduce their command sequences in a report or in a fitness-app agent. Your empirical protocol is black-box HTTP against Docker fitness apps, not Linux privilege escalation. What transfers is the **loop shape**: model → authorized action → observed output → next model turn, plus a log that later lets you see whether a claim was grounded in output or in training-set fan-fiction.

Prototype code being public is a dual-use fact they return to in §6. Your new AutoPT agent should similarly be a thin orchestrator with a localhost kill switch, not a copy of their Linux privesc driver.

### 4.1 Grounding of results and hallucinations

Because every command and its output are logged, they can ask: did the next suggestion *follow* from data, or from priors? After sudoers output, suggestions stayed in the sudo family. After passwd, suggestions moved to weak local accounts. That is causal-looking. Other suggestions (famous kernel-class issues named from the mere fact the OS is Linux) arrived **without** enumeration. Rare, obvious hallucinations included asking to run a fictional `exploit.sh` that probably existed in write-ups. They compare the whole experience to phoning a clever colleague who cannot see the screen.

When asked to explain findings, GPT-3.5 produced decent teaching prose — a training aid, not an oracle. For your RQ2: keep the same log discipline. If the agent names broken object-level authorization after seeing two users’ JSON, that is grounded. If it names it from the README alone, that is a prior. If it names a tool that does not exist, that is a hallucination. Happe already required that distinction in 2023.

### 4.2 Stability and reproducibility

Single runs wander. Aggregate tens of runs and the *distribution* of behaviours converges. They analogise to testers falling down rabbit holes; experience is what pulls humans out. Compared with linpeas-style enumerators (hard-coded checklists), LLMs are less deterministic — maybe that even helps against naive IDS pattern matching, they speculate, which is not a reason for you to go unattended on a university network. One run even tried to fetch linpeas from a **bad URL**, a tiny comedy of grounded tool choice plus hallucinated location.

For a five-app study, this subsection is why you **cap calls per app** and why you do not treat one overnight trace as destiny. Repeatability is a table of k/n apps, not a single lucky shell.
